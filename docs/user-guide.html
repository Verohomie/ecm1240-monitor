ecm1240-monitor — User Guide

Version 1.0 Updated: 2026-07-28 Using the dashboard, the charts, and the insights
Every example here uses made-up data. The circuit names are the ordinary things people meter — a fridge, a water heater, a washer. The numbers come from the demo generator, not from anyone's home. Do the same when you post a screenshot: run tools/make_demo_data.py rather than sharing your own, because a real chart shows when your house is empty.

The Dashboard

Three parts: the whole-house figure, a card per circuit, and any findings.

Dashboard anatomy. Circuit names come from your config file, not from the code.
What you seeWhat it means
Whole house Your mains channel if you have a CT on the service. If you don't, it is the sum of the metered circuits instead, and the label changes to "Metered Total" — because a sum cannot see anything without a clamp on it.
The cost line Current draw projected forward at your configured rate. It answers "if things stayed exactly like this". It is not a bill prediction.
Circuit card Live watts on that circuit. The bar is its share of whichever circuit is busiest at that moment, so bars move around — it is a glance-comparison, not a percentage of the house.
"hidden" tag A channel with hidden: true in your config. Still recorded, greyed out — useful for a CT you suspect but do not want to lose history on.
"last reading Ns ago" Anything over a minute means the collector has stopped or a meter has gone quiet. This is the line that tells you the system is broken.

The Chart

Tap the whole-house figure or any circuit card. The chart is a real address (#/chart/0/aux1), so Back and a touch back-swipe both work.

Chart anatomy. The blue block on the strip is the part of the range you are looking at.
GestureDoes
Drag across the chartZoom into that span.
Drag the strip's blue blockPan — same width of time, moved earlier or later.
Drag the block's edgesWiden or narrow the window.
Scroll / pinchZoom around the pointer.
Reset zoomBack to the whole selected range. Only appears when zoomed.
Back button / swipeReturns to the dashboard.

Why zooming actually shows more

This is the part worth understanding, because it is what makes the chart useful rather than decorative. Most dashboards fetch a fixed number of points and then stretch them when you zoom, so magnifying a smooth line just gives you a bigger smooth line. This one throws the old data away and asks the server again for just the window you want.

Practically: if something looks odd on a 24-hour view, zoom into it. The detail is in the database the whole time — the chart just was not asking for it.

Insights

Rules that watch each circuit's behaviour and say something in plain language. They are opt-in: nothing appears until you have generated profiles.yaml from your own data, because the thresholds depend on your appliances.

KindUse it forWhat it watches
cycling Fridge, freezer, air handler, heat pump — anything with a compressor Short-cycling (repeated brief runs, hard on a compressor), starts per hour, an unbroken run that has gone on too long, and duty cycle.
multistate Variable-speed pumps, multi-speed fans Reports the distinct power levels it found, and flags never switching off.
burst Range, washer, dryer, outlets, workshop Only a stuck-on state. Being idle for days is normal for a human-driven circuit, so silence is never reported as a fault.
always_on Networking gear, a media rack, anything expected to hum along The steady floor itself — flags it dropping (something lost power) or climbing (something new was added).

Reading a card

ColourMeans
ALERTSomething is behaving in a way that usually indicates a fault. Worth acting on.
WATCHUnusual but often explainable — a hot day, a party, a long wash. Worth knowing, not alarming.
LEARNINGNot enough history yet to judge drift. It says so rather than guessing.
OKBehaving as expected, with the evidence shown so you can sanity-check the rule.
The rules describe behaviour, not diagnosis. "Short-cycling" means short runs happened, which is genuinely worth investigating. It does not know whether that is a dirty filter, low refrigerant, or a party where the door kept opening. Treat a card as a reason to go and look.

The Charts — How They Work, and Swapping In a Library

The charts are hand-written SVG in web/index.html, with no charting library. That is a deliberate choice with a real trade-off, so here is the reasoning, what you give up, and exactly how to swap in a library if you would rather.

Why no charting library

ReasonDetail
Auditability The main one. This software runs on a machine inside your home and records when your house is occupied. A minified bundle is roughly 700 KB of unreadable JavaScript that nobody can meaningfully review, and you would be trusting it with that. The whole front end here is one file you can read.
Size A vendored chart library is larger than this entire project — source, docs and history combined. That is a strange shape for a repository.
Offline No CDN, so it works with the internet unplugged. (A vendored copy also achieves this — it is only a reason against loading from a CDN.)
Licence hygiene Bundling third-party code means carrying its licence and notices correctly. Not hard, but it is real work and easy to get subtly wrong.

What you give up — honestly

MissingImpact
Crosshair / tooltip readout The clearest gap. You cannot hover a point and read its exact value and time. Average, peak and total are shown for the visible window instead. This is on the roadmap.
Animated transitions Zooming snaps rather than glides. Cosmetic.
Multi-series overlays One circuit per chart. Comparing two circuits, or overlaying a second axis (a thermostat setpoint, say), would need real work here whereas a library gives it to you.
Battle-tested edge cases A mature library has handled years of odd browsers and odd data. This is a few hundred lines that works well in current browsers.
NOT missing Drag-to-zoom, pan, edge-resize, wheel/pinch zoom, and the re-fetch-at-higher-resolution behaviour are all implemented. Those were the point.

How the chart is built

All of it lives in web/index.html. Four moving parts:

PieceNameJob
StateCHWhich circuit is open, the base range from the buttons, the view currently on screen, and the two datasets.
FetchloadOverview()
loadDetail()		overview is the whole range at low resolution for the strip and only changes when you press a range button. detail is the visible window and is re-fetched on every zoom or pan. Requests carry a sequence number so a slow reply can never overwrite a newer one.
Drawdraw()Builds the whole SVG as a string — gridlines, the line and fill path, axis labels, the strip and its selection block.
InputwireInteractions()Pointer events (one code path for mouse, touch and pen) for drag-select, wheel zoom, and dragging the strip block or its edges.

The only API call the chart makes

GET /api/history?channel=aux3&unit=0&minutes=318&end=1785259163&points=900

{ "channel": "aux3", "unit": 0, "bucket_s": 20,
  "start": 1785240083, "end": 1785259163,
  "points": [ {"ts": 1785240083, "w": 148.2}, ... ] }

end is what makes zooming work — it lets you ask for a window that does not run up to now. points is how many buckets you want back; the server divides the window by that and averages each bucket, so asking for more points over a smaller window is exactly how you get finer resolution. Any chart library you drop in talks to this same endpoint.

Swapping in a chart library

Entirely reasonable if you want tooltips and multi-series. The data layer does not change — only draw() and wireInteractions() get replaced. Using Apache ECharts as the example, since it handles zoom well:

  • 1 Vendor the library. Download echarts.min.js into web/ and reference it locally — <script src="/echarts.min.js"></script>. Do not use a CDN: it breaks when your internet is down and it tells a third party every time you look at your own dashboard.
  • 2 Handle the licence. ECharts is Apache-2.0, which is compatible with this project's MIT licence but has conditions: keep the copyright and licence headers intact, include the full Apache-2.0 text (as LICENSE-APACHE-2.0), and add a NOTICE file. Also add it to CREDITS.md and remove the "no JavaScript dependencies" claim from README.md and CREDITS.md, which will no longer be true.
  • 3 Replace draw(). Initialise once per circuit and call setOption() with CH.detail mapped to [ts*1000, w] pairs. Add dataZoom with both an inside (scroll/pinch) and a slider (the strip) component, and a tooltip with trigger: 'axis' — that is the readout you were missing.
  • 4 Keep the re-fetch. This is the part worth preserving. Listen for dataZoom events, convert the resulting percentage range back into timestamps, write them into CH.view, and call loadDetail(). Debounce it — a scroll-zoom fires continuously and you do not want a request per frame. Without this step the library will happily magnify the data already loaded and you will have swapped a working feature for a prettier version of nothing.
  • 5 Rebuild rather than reuse when switching circuits if each circuit gets its own colour — reusing one instance across circuits tends to keep the old series' styling.
  • 6 Watch the resolution trap. If you add any caching of history responses, key it on the resolution as well as the channel and window. Keying on channel and window alone means whoever asks first wins — a small request for a thumbnail gets handed to a chart that wanted a thousand points, and it silently draws at a fraction of the detail it asked for.
Either choice is defensible. The project ships dependency-free because that is the better default for software running inside someone's home. If you want richer charts on your own install and you are comfortable with a vendored bundle, the swap is contained: one drawing function, one event handler, and the licence paperwork.

Questions People Ask

QuestionAnswer
Why don't the circuits add up to the whole house? They shouldn't. The mains sees everything; the circuits only see what has a clamp on it. The difference is your unmetered load. If the circuits add up to more than the mains, that is a real problem — see CALIBRATION.md.
Why does a channel read a few watts when everything is off? Normal at the bottom of a CT's range. If several idle channels on one meter drift together, that is shared noise rather than real load — don't add those up and treat the total as real.
Where do watts come from? The meter doesn't send them. Correct — it sends a cumulative energy counter. The software divides the change in that counter by the change in the meter's own clock. That's why two packets are always needed before any reading appears, and why a brief oddity right after a data gap is a timing artifact rather than a real spike.
Can I add more circuits later? Yes. Add the CT, add the channel to your config, restart. History for existing channels is untouched.
How much disk does this use? Roughly 1 GB per meter per year at the default cadence. Trivial for a drive, fatal for an SD card — which is why the database belongs on real storage.
Can I see it from work? Use a VPN. Do not port-forward it — there is no authentication, and the data shows when your house is empty.
Insights say nothing. They need profiles.yaml, generated from your own data after a few days. There are no built-in thresholds because someone else's would produce confident nonsense about your appliances.
